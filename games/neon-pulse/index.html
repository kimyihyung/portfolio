How to play

Four lanes, one rule: hit the note when it crosses the line

Neon Pulse is a rhythm game first and a 3D scene second. Bars of light race down four neon lanes at 128 BPM, and every one that crosses the hit line is a note of the melody — a note only you can play. Miss it and the melody goes quiet, the combo breaks, and the floor stops pulsing to your inputs.

Keyboard

D F J K hit lanes 1–4, the classic four-key layout. The 1–4 number row and the arrow keys work identically. Space or Enter starts and restarts, P or Esc pauses, M mutes.

Touch

On a phone, four large zones appear at the bottom of the screen, one per lane. Tap them in rhythm — or tap anywhere on the play field itself, which is divided into the same four lanes. Nothing to hold, nothing to drag.

Mouse

On a desktop without a keyboard handy, click the quarter of the play field that matches the lane. It is slower than the keys — the keys are the intended way to play — but it works for a first look.

A run is 90 seconds: a two-bar count-in, a warm-up, verses, a break, a second chorus and a final chord. There is no health bar and no fail state — the score at the end is the whole report.

Scoring

What the numbers actually mean

Every note is judged by how close your hit lands to the beat: within ±52 ms is Perfect, ±100 ms Great, ±150 ms Good, and past that the note counts as a Miss and breaks the combo. The multiplier grows with the combo and multiplies everything.

Every 25 comboa chime marks the milestone

Accuracy weighs a Perfect as 100%, a Great as 65% and a Good as 30%. The end-of-run rank is S from 96% with no misses, A from 90%, B from 80%, C from 65%, and D for everything else.

Strategy

Five habits that move a score from 300k to 700k

  1. Play the melody, do not chase the bars

    The chart follows an A minor pentatonic line. If you can hum the phrase, your hands already know when to move — listening beats watching the lanes, especially in the chorus where the bars arrive faster than the eye.

  2. Anchor your fingers, do not lift them

    Rest D F J K over the four lanes and press instead of reaching. Lifting and re-finding keys is where streaks die; the tiny movements cost less than the travel.

  3. Protect the multiplier early

    The multiplier needs 12 combo for its first step and 84 for the last. A missed Easy bar early in the verse costs more than a shaky chorus later, because every point after it earns less.

  4. Learn the chart — it is the same every time

    The bars are generated once from a fixed seed, so section by section the song repeats exactly. After three or four runs you will feel the build-up roll coming and pre-load your fingers.

  5. Pick the difficulty that keeps the combo alive

    Hard adds sixteenth-note bursts and speeds the floor up, and its multiplier steps every 14 instead of 12. A clean 96% Normal run outscores a scrappy Hard run almost every time — move up when Normal stops being tense.

Inside the build

A concert hall with no files in it

There is not a single audio file, image or network call in the game itself. The track is scheduled oscillator by oscillator on the Web Audio clock, the stage is a shader-drawn neon floor, and the notes are one instanced mesh. Three.js ships as a local file next to the page — no CDN, no tracker, nothing uploaded anywhere.

Synthesised track

Kick, snare, hats, bass, pads and arpeggios are scheduled up to 200 ms ahead of time with the Web Audio clock, and your hits play the pentatonic melody on top. A generated impulse gives the whole thing one small reverb.

Shader floor

The lanes, the scrolling grid and the beat pulse are one GLSL fragment shader on a single plane. The pulse you see under a perfect hit is the same value that drives the horizon glow.

Seeded chart

Each difficulty draws its bars from a fixed seed and stores nothing but your score. The same 90 seconds plays on every device, so records are comparable and the chart can be learned.

Rendering is Three.js r169 — bundled with the page, not fetched from a CDN. Notes are one instanced mesh with a bright core pass; hit bursts come from a pool of twenty sprites allocated at boot, so nothing is created mid-run. If the frame rate dips, the renderer lowers its resolution a step before you feel it.

FAQ

Questions about the game

Is Neon Pulse really free?

Yes. There is no account, no install, no in-game purchase and nothing uploaded. The page is a static file served from GitHub Pages.

How do I play on a phone?

Four large touch zones sit at the bottom of the screen, one per lane, and the play field itself is also divided into four tappable lanes. The layout was checked at 390 × 844 and holds without sideways scrolling; iOS Safari and Chrome for Android both run it.

Which keys do I use on a computer?

D F J K for the four lanes — the classic layout — or 1–4 and the arrow keys. Space starts, P pauses, M mutes.

Where is my high score stored?

In this browser only, under localStorage keys beginning with neon-pulse — one per difficulty. Nothing is transmitted: there is no analytics script, no cookie and no server to send anything to. Clearing site data clears the record.

Does it need an internet connection?

Only to load the page the first time. Three.js is bundled with the page and every sound is synthesised on your device, so once loaded the game makes no requests at all — no CDN, no tracker, no calls of any kind.

Is the chart always the same?

Yes. Each difficulty has a fixed, seeded chart, so the song is identical everywhere and every run. A score means the same thing for everyone, and the chart can be learned like a real rhythm game.

What is it built with?

Three.js r169 for rendering, bundled locally; a GLSL shader for the floor and the beat pulse; the Web Audio API for the entire soundtrack. There are no audio files, no image files and no network calls in the game. More hand-built toys live at /portfolio/tools/, and the first game, Celestial Glide, is a dodge racer in a shader nebula.
