How the frame works

measured on a canvas

01Cover, then move

100% zoom means the image covers the frame with no gaps: the scale is the larger of frame-width ÷ image-width and frame-height ÷ image-height. Dragging changes an offset that is clamped to the overhang, so you can never pull an empty corner into the crop. The slider multiplies that cover scale, which is why 200% gives half the field of view rather than twice the picture size.

02Padding, blur and solid

Padding is a share of the frame's shorter side, so the border looks the same on 720 px and 2048 px. The inset leaves a border that is filled first — with a blurred, over-scaled copy of the same photo (radius ≈ 2.2% of width, painted 16% larger so the blur has no transparent rim) or with one flat colour. Then the photo itself is drawn inside the inset at the same rules as above.

03One decode, one encode

The file is decoded once, drawn at the output size, and encoded once — PNG for lossless (alpha kept), WebP for the smallest file at a given quality, JPG when something else must open it (alpha flattened to white). The number under the download button is the byte length of that encode, and it moves when the image moves, because a crop with less detail really is smaller.

Questions

crop · pad · export
Does cropping re-compress my photo?
Once, and only once. The file is decoded to pixels, the crop is drawn onto a fresh canvas at the output size, and that canvas is encoded in the format you pick. A PNG export of a PNG stays lossless; a JPG export is one generation of JPEG at the quality you set — which is why quality 92 is the default and why the size is shown before you download.
Which ratio do I need?
1:1 for a profile picture and most marketplace listings, 4:5 for the tallest post Instagram's feed will show without cropping, 9:16 for Reels, Shorts and Stories, 16:9 for a YouTube thumbnail, 1.91:1 for a link preview card. If a platform asks for 1080 × 1350, that is 4:5 at 1080 wide — pick 4:5, set the output width to 1080, and the height comes out to 1350 exactly.
Why does the crop cut off something I wanted?
Because a ratio only keeps what fits. Zoom out to 100% and drag to choose which part is kept, or add padding: at 12–16% on a 9:16 frame the whole photo fits inside a blurred border, which is the usual fix for a landscape photo going into a vertical story. Centre puts the frame back in the middle of the image without touching your zoom.
Where do the saved files go?
Straight to your browser's normal downloads — named after the ratio and the real pixel size, for example imgcrop-16x9-1080x608.png. Nothing is written to a server, a cookie or local storage: the footer counts external requests and it stays at 0.
